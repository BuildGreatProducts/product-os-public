# BONUS - Business Models

The sixteen ways an app business can earn, one section each. Read by `define-pricing` when it fills section 2 of your Pricing Strategy (*How the Business Earns*); useful on its own whenever you are choosing, questioning, or changing how the money arrives.

Start from your Product Offer: **customer, pain, outcome, mechanism, proof, guarantee**. The business model is the answer to *who pays, and for what relationship or value* — it is not the billing unit (seat, document, location), not the packaging (tiers, add-ons), and not the entry route (free tier, trial, pilot). Those live in [BONUS - Pricing Models](BONUS-Pricing-Models.md). Pick **one primary model**; add a secondary only if it earns its place.

## Start from how the value arrives

| The value is… | Start with |
| --- | --- |
| Continuous, and recognised again at every renewal | [Subscription](#business-01-subscription) |
| One bounded result the customer keeps | [One-time purchase](#business-02-one-time-purchase), [Lifetime offer](#business-16-lifetime-offers) |
| Proportional to how much they consume | [Usage-based](#business-03-usage-based), [Credits / consumables](#business-04-credits-consumables) |
| Extensions on a base product they already own | [Paid content / add-ons](#business-05-paid-content-add-ons) |
| A completed exchange between two sides | [Transaction / commission](#business-06-transaction-commission) |
| Attention a third party will pay to reach | [Ads / sponsorship](#business-07-ads-sponsorship), [Affiliate / lead generation](#business-08-affiliate-lead-generation) |
| Bought by an organisation with procurement | [Enterprise contracts](#business-09-enterprise-contracts), [White-label / OEM](#business-10-white-label-oem) |
| Free software, paid convenience or expertise | [Open-core / hosting](#business-11-open-core-hosting), [Software + services](#business-12-software-services) |
| A verified result | [Outcome-based](#business-13-outcome-based) |
| Goodwill from a community | [Donations / patronage](#business-14-donations-patronage) |
| Indirect: it feeds another business you run | [Supports another business](#business-15-supports-another-business) |

Some categories overlap on purpose: usage and outcomes describe both a revenue family and a billable unit; enterprise describes a commercial relationship; open-core describes what is free versus paid. They are practical ways to design a business, not mutually exclusive boxes.

Freemium and trials need a revenue model behind them. "AI-powered" describes a capability. A guarantee reduces purchase risk. None of these alone tells you how the business earns.

**Scope.** App fit, distribution suggestions, and experiments are recommendations to validate, not guarantees. Every number is a hypothetical planning example, excludes tax, and is not a market benchmark. Sources support the specific mechanism or product example beside them; they do not establish the right price for your app.

<a id="business-01-subscription"></a>

## 1. Subscription

**How it works:** Customers pay repeatedly for continued access or recurring value. The renewal is a fresh purchase decision: the app must remain useful after the first successful session. Subscription describes the revenue relationship; you can still price per account, seat, or usage.

**Best-fit apps:** Fitness and learning apps with continuing programmes; productivity tools used each week; monitoring, hosting, and team workflows. Weak fit for a task customers finish once and never repeat.

**What tends to work:** Make the first useful result happen early. Then surface meaningful progress: completed work, time saved, goals reached, or problems prevented. Use reminders and streaks only when they support the customer's objective. Stored history can make the product more useful; provide export rather than relying on customers feeling trapped.

**What tends to fail:** Discounts can postpone cancellation without fixing low value. New features, daily notifications, and a yearly commitment do not by themselves create retention. Avoid funding expensive unlimited AI usage from a low fixed subscription.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: 100 customers at $20/month produce $2,000 monthly recurring revenue before costs. At $4 direct delivery cost per customer, $1,600 remains before acquisition and fixed overhead. Losing 8 customers means $160 lost recurring revenue unless expansion or new sales replaces it.

**What to measure:** Track activation, repeat core-action use, customer retention by signup cohort, cancellations, and contribution per retained customer. Annual cash collected is not twelve new months of demand. Review renewal cohorts separately.

**Distribution implications:** Start with problem-led SEO or tutorials for recurring workflows; ASO and short-form demonstrations for consumer habits; targeted outreach for costly business problems. Paid acquisition needs evidence customers stay long enough to recover its cost.

**First experiment:** Ask: 'What will still be worth paying for in month six?' Then test one value-summary email against a retained-usage metric, rather than testing a discount first.

**Further reading:** Apple describes subscriptions as access that can renew until cancellation. See [Apple's monetization overview](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/business-models/).

<a id="business-02-one-time-purchase"></a>

## 2. One-time purchase

**How it works:** A customer pays once for a defined product, licence, or fixed deliverable. Define what they keep, which version or features they receive, and whether updates, hosting, and support are included. A fixed-price project promising a result is still a purchase; it becomes outcome billing only when payment depends on qualifying results.

**Best-fit apps:** Desktop utilities, offline tools, premium games, specialist calculators, and bounded transformation products. Attractive when customers dislike another subscription and ongoing servicing costs are low.

**What tends to work:** Sell a concrete outcome and demonstrate it before checkout. Price support and maintenance into the offer. A higher price can be justified by a more valuable result or better evidence, rather than simply by adding features.

**What tends to fail:** Low pricing can leave no room for acquisition or support. Do not promise permanently hosted services at a price calculated only from today's server bill. 'Pay once' must not conceal a required second purchase.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: a $79 sale minus $5 payment/refund allowance, $14 support reserve, and $20 acquisition cost leaves $40 before development and overhead. The reserve is a planning assumption, not a guarantee of future cost.

**What to measure:** Track contribution per sale, refund reasons, support hours per purchaser, and paid-upgrade adoption. Measure the whole customer cohort's future cost rather than only launch-week cash.

**Distribution implications:** High-intent search, app-store discovery, demonstrations, reviews, and relevant newsletters can communicate a clear one-off job. Affiliates need a commission the sale can actually support.

**First experiment:** Compare two offers: a $19 generic utility and a $79 tool for an expensive specialist task. Ask what evidence makes the latter credible. Test a scoped offer with qualified prospects before assuming the lowest price wins.

**Further reading:** Apple's paid-app model is a single download purchase. Platform purchase terminology does not determine your complete support promise. See [Apple's model descriptions](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/business-models/).

<a id="business-03-usage-based"></a>

## 3. Usage-based

**How it works:** Revenue increases with consumption: documents processed, storage used, minutes transcribed, or requests delivered. It can be prepaid or billed afterwards. Usage is both a business-model family here and a billing unit in `BONUS-Pricing-Models.md`.

**Best-fit apps:** APIs, infrastructure, document automation, transcription, and variable-volume AI tools. Best when the billed unit is understandable and reasonably aligned with both customer value and delivery cost.

**What tends to work:** Show examples of a small, normal, and heavy bill. Provide visible consumption, budget alerts, and explicit rules for retries and failures. Let the customer estimate cost before a large job. Choose a unit they can explain internally.

**What tends to fail:** Charging for invisible internal tokens can confuse a buyer who thinks in documents. Uncapped usage creates surprise bills; price caps without cost controls create surprise losses. Optimising a customer's usage can reduce your revenue, so plan for that incentive.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: 2,000 accepted documents at $0.08 each produce $160. If processing, retries, and review cost $0.03 per accepted document, contribution is $100 before account support, acquisition, and fixed costs.

**What to measure:** Track billable units, cost per successful unit, failed/retried work, usage concentration, and contribution per account. Heavy customers may be your least profitable if discounted rates undercut serving costs.

**Distribution implications:** Technical tutorials, integration documentation, SEO, developer communities, and targeted business outreach let prospects test a real workload. Broad lifestyle content rarely explains a specialist billing unit well.

**First experiment:** Run a shadow invoice on a real sample workload and ask the customer to predict it first. The gap reveals whether your unit and explanation are clear.

**Further reading:** Stripe distinguishes pay-as-you-go, fixed-fee-plus-overage, and credit-based implementations. See [usage billing documentation](https://docs.stripe.com/billing/usage-based).

<a id="business-04-credits-consumables"></a>

## 4. Credits / consumables

**How it works:** Customers buy units that are depleted as they use the app. Credits can represent a simple job allowance or cover several operations at different exchange rates. Cash arrives before delivery, but you still owe the promised work.

**Best-fit apps:** Image, video, and document generation; occasional processing tasks; games with optional consumable items. Useful for sporadic customers who do not want recurring commitments.

**What tends to work:** Translate credits into examples: how many ordinary exports, generations, or minutes the pack buys. Show each action's cost before confirmation. Define expiry, rollover, refunds, and failed-job restoration clearly.

**What tends to fail:** Complicated exchange rates obscure value. Bundles that strand tiny unusable balances frustrate customers. Expensive generation models can make old credit promises costly; plan pricing changes before selling large long-lived packs.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: $30 buys 100 standard jobs. At $0.12 expected cost per redeemed job, full redemption costs $12, leaving $18 before fees and support. Stress-test full redemption rather than assuming customers forget their balance.

**What to measure:** Track repeat purchase, redemption timing, outstanding balances, failed jobs, and cost per credit redeemed. Separate cash collection from completed service when planning.

**Distribution implications:** Short demos and creator partnerships can sell visible outputs; SEO and tutorials fit specific jobs. Measure paid repeat usage, not only free-credit signups.

**First experiment:** Put the credit wallet beside three common jobs and ask a customer to convert it back into understandable units. Pilot a small pack and observe whether customers buy again after using it.

**Further reading:** Consumables are depleted and can be purchased again in [Apple's purchase taxonomy](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/business-models/).

<a id="business-05-paid-content-add-ons"></a>

## 5. Paid content / add-ons

**How it works:** Sell an additional module, pack, lesson, template, integration, or capability to a customer who already has the base product. The extra can be a permanent purchase or recurring access; those terms must be explicit.

**Best-fit apps:** Creative tools, learning apps, games, specialist professional software, and modular business workflows. Best when different customers need genuinely different extensions.

**What tends to work:** Write a mini Product Offer for each purchase: specific customer, pain, outcome, mechanism, proof, and guarantee. Introduce the offer where the customer encounters that need. Keep the base product capable of delivering its own advertised outcome.

**What tends to fail:** Splitting essential steps into surprise charges damages the original promise. Too many tiny modules make the buying decision difficult and increase support. Bundles can hide weak standalone value.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: 1,000 active customers × 8% buying a $25 pack = $2,000 sales before costs. This is an assumption-driven scenario, not an expected attachment rate. Include pack production, licensing, updates, and support costs.

**What to measure:** Track the percentage of eligible customers who buy, incremental contribution, usage after purchase, and refunds. A purchase with no use is weak evidence for the next pack.

**Distribution implications:** In-app recommendations, customer email, tutorial videos, and specialist creator demonstrations reach people already experiencing the relevant problem. Customer education can outperform another broad launch.

**First experiment:** Demonstrate the base outcome first, then a distinct upgrade outcome. Test one focused pack with existing customers before creating a large catalogue.

**Further reading:** Apple describes paymium as a paid download with optional further purchases. See [Apple's business-model overview](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/business-models/).

<a id="business-06-transaction-commission"></a>

## 6. Transaction / commission

**How it works:** Earn a fee when a buyer and seller complete a transaction through your product. You facilitate exchange rather than owning all the goods or services. A fixed transaction fee and a percentage take rate are pricing options within this family.

**Best-fit apps:** Booking platforms, service marketplaces, commerce marketplaces, and payments-enabled vertical software. Strongest when the app materially improves discovery, trust, coordination, or payment.

**What tends to work:** Start with one narrow category or location and solve matching manually if necessary. Give both sides a reason to remain on-platform after meeting. Define responsibility for cancellations, disputes, and service quality.

**What tends to fail:** Two empty sides do not create a marketplace. Gross transaction volume is not revenue. A large take rate invites bypassing if the app adds little ongoing value. Refunds and support can consume a thin fee.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: $50,000 completed transaction value × 8% platform fee = $4,000 fee revenue before payment expenses, refunds, support, and acquisition. If only $2,500 remains after direct costs, use that figure when planning growth.

**What to measure:** Track match rate, time to match, repeat transactions, take rate, dispute cost, and contribution per completed order. Check buyer and seller retention separately.

**Distribution implications:** Direct outreach can recruit the scarce side; niche communities, local search, and partnerships can seed demand. Avoid spending broadly before supply can fulfil the promise.

**First experiment:** Draw two separate funnels, one for each side. Manually complete ten matches and record why each succeeded or failed before automating.

**Further reading:** Stripe Connect supports marketplace payment flows and platform monetization; it does not solve demand or supply. See [Stripe's marketplace overview](https://stripe.com/connect/marketplaces).

<a id="business-07-ads-sponsorship"></a>

## 7. Ads / sponsorship

**How it works:** Users receive an experience funded by advertisers or sponsors. Display advertising sells measurable exposure or actions; direct sponsorship sells access to a relevant audience and may include custom placements.

**Best-fit apps:** Media, discovery tools, games, and high-frequency consumer apps. A small specialist audience may support direct sponsorship even when display-ad revenue would be modest.

**What tends to work:** Build useful repeat attention and match advertisers to genuine audience interests. Choose placements that preserve the core task. Sell sponsors a clear audience, placement, duration, and reporting promise.

**What tends to fail:** Downloads without repeat use create little inventory. Adding more ads can reduce retention. Cheap paid traffic is not useful if advertising yield is lower than acquisition and servicing costs. Sponsor concentration makes revenue fragile.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: 100,000 monetized impressions at $4 effective revenue per thousand yield $400 before app costs. For sponsorship, two $500 placements yield $1,000, but selling and production time must also be counted. These are invented rates.

**What to measure:** Track monetized impressions, fill rate, net revenue per thousand, retention after ad exposure, and contribution per active user. For sponsors, track repeat bookings and relevant downstream actions.

**Distribution implications:** SEO, social content, and shareable utility can grow audiences. Direct outreach to tightly matched sponsors is a separate sales motion from acquiring app users.

**First experiment:** Compare the audience size needed for $1,000 from display ads with one specialist sponsorship. Test a small placement and monitor whether it harms the user's task.

**Further reading:** Apple identifies advertising-funded free apps and warns that poor ad experiences can affect engagement. See [Apple's monetization overview](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/business-models/).

<a id="business-08-affiliate-lead-generation"></a>

## 8. Affiliate / lead generation

**How it works:** Your app earns money by sending qualified customers or leads to another business. Affiliate commissions usually depend on a tracked downstream action; lead-generation fees depend on agreed lead criteria. The advertiser or provider is the paying customer.

**Best-fit apps:** Comparison tools, product discovery, eligibility checkers, calculators, and matching apps that help users make a purchase decision.

**What tends to work:** Deliver a useful result first, then suggest a relevant next step. Agree qualification, attribution windows, duplicate handling, and payout terms with the buyer of the lead. Make commercial relationships clear and preserve recommendation quality.

**What tends to fail:** Recommending the highest commission instead of the best fit erodes trust. A click may never produce a paid commission. One partner can change eligibility or stop the programme. Do not confuse this model with rewarding customers to refer users to your own paid app; that is distribution.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: 1,000 relevant outbound visits × 3% approved purchase rate × $40 commission = $1,200 before content, app, and acquisition costs. Rejected or reversed conversions reduce the result.

**What to measure:** Track approved earnings per outbound visitor, rejection/reversal rates, payout delays, partner concentration, and user satisfaction with recommendations.

**Distribution implications:** High-intent SEO, comparison videos, specialist newsletters, and useful community answers suit research-stage customers. Avoid presenting paid rankings as independent tests.

**First experiment:** Map 'result → relevant recommendation → qualified action → approved payout'. Validate one partner and a small set of useful referrals before building a large comparison database.

**Further reading:** Shopify's programme rewards qualified merchant referrals, illustrating that eligibility matters more than raw traffic. See [Shopify Affiliates](https://www.shopify.com/affiliates).

<a id="business-09-enterprise-contracts"></a>

## 9. Enterprise contracts

**How it works:** Sell a negotiated package to an organisation: software access plus the governance, deployment, support, and purchasing arrangements it needs. This is a commercial sales model that can combine subscriptions, seats, usage, and services.

**Best-fit apps:** Team workflows, analytics, infrastructure, and business-critical systems with organisational buyers. A focused indie product can serve this market if its scope and support commitments are manageable.

**What tends to work:** Identify the user, internal champion, budget owner, and approvers. Prove a specific business result in a bounded pilot. Prepare clear security answers, implementation scope, support boundaries, and a success plan.

**What tends to fail:** Long sales cycles can exhaust a solo founder's runway. Bespoke commitments create delivery obligations before revenue arrives. Enterprise customers do not automatically make a business fundable or easy to sell; retention, margins, concentration, and transferability still matter.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: a $24,000 annual contract minus $5,000 direct hosting/support and $4,000 onboarding leaves $15,000 before sales cost and overhead in year one. Count founder time spent securing it.

**What to measure:** Track qualified pipeline, sales-cycle length, pilot-to-contract conversion, implementation effort, renewal, and revenue concentration. Signed contract value and cash received are different.

**Distribution implications:** Targeted outreach, professional communities, partner introductions, case studies, and long-form demonstrations can reach specific buyers. Broad content helps only when it attracts the right roles.

**First experiment:** Map the four-person buying committee before you design a checkout button. Offer a paid pilot with acceptance criteria and a decision date.

**Further reading:** GitLab combines named plans with enterprise purchasing options; Metabase documents governance and implementation support. See [GitLab pricing](https://about.gitlab.com/pricing/) and [Metabase Enterprise](https://www.metabase.com/product/enterprise).

<a id="business-10-white-label-oem"></a>

## 10. White-label / OEM

**How it works:** Another company sells your software under its brand or embeds it within its product. White-label usually changes branding; OEM arrangements incorporate your capability into a wider offering. The partner often owns the end-customer relationship.

**Best-fit apps:** Agency platforms, embedded reporting, specialist workflow engines, and industry software that partners can sell to an existing customer base.

**What tends to work:** Keep one configurable product instead of a separate codebase per partner. Price onboarding separately when it requires work. Define usage reporting, branding rights, support responsibilities, renewal, and what happens when the partnership ends.

**What tends to fail:** One large partner can dominate revenue and the roadmap. 'Fewer customers' can mean fewer invoices but many downstream users to support. Faster revenue is possible, not inherent; integration and negotiation may be slow.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: five partners at $600/month yield $3,000 recurring revenue. Losing one removes 20%. Include end-user volume and integration support when calculating cost, not just partner count.

**What to measure:** Track partner activation, downstream usage, expansion, support load, and concentration. A signed reseller that never launches is not a functioning channel.

**Distribution implications:** Targeted partner outreach, agency communities, industry events, and integration demos suit this model. Supply sales material the partner can reuse.

**First experiment:** Demonstrate the same app under two customer brands. Pilot with one partner and a fixed integration scope before promising exclusivity or custom features.

**Further reading:** Metabase offers embedded analytics and white-label presentation, illustrating the capability layer. See [Metabase embedded analytics](https://www.metabase.com/product/embedded-analytics).

<a id="business-11-open-core-hosting"></a>

## 11. Open-core / hosting

**How it works:** Offer openly licensed software while earning from complementary value. Open-core reserves some capabilities for paid editions; managed hosting sells operational convenience; paid support sells expertise. These approaches can combine but are not interchangeable.

**Best-fit apps:** Developer tools, infrastructure, technical automation, and products whose users value inspectability or self-hosting. The paying buyer may be an organisation that wants to avoid operating the software itself.

**What tends to work:** Make the free installation useful, document it well, and explain the paid upgrade clearly in the repository and product. Sell saved operational effort, governance, or support. Keep community expectations aligned with the actual licence and product boundary.

**What tends to fail:** Stars and downloads do not imply paid demand. Community support has a real cost. Public source code is not automatically open source, and you must understand the licence before promising redistribution or embedding rights.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: 50 hosted accounts at $30/month yield $1,500. Subtract infrastructure, support, and maintenance; volunteer contributions should not be assumed in the delivery plan.

**What to measure:** Track activated installations where measurable, hosted conversion, paid retention, contribution per hosted account, and support time. Respect self-hosted users' measurement preferences.

**Distribution implications:** Repository discovery, tutorials, technical SEO, integration examples, and developer communities can build trust. Give a natural path from self-hosted experimentation to a managed service.

**First experiment:** Study Postiz: repository, installation path, and hosted offer. Ask why someone who can self-host would choose to pay, then ask the same question of your own product.

**Further reading:** Postiz's repository identifies its AGPL-3.0 licence and links to its hosted product. See [Postiz repository](https://github.com/gitroomhq/postiz-app). This confirms its source availability and licence, not an assumption about which features are proprietary.

<a id="business-12-software-services"></a>

## 12. Software + services

**How it works:** Customers buy software together with human setup, migration, training, implementation, or ongoing expertise. The software can improve a service business's delivery, while service work helps customers reach the app's outcome.

**Best-fit apps:** Automation, data migration, reporting, and industry-specific workflows. Also fits consultants or service owners who already understand a recurring customer problem.

**What tends to work:** Productise a narrow scope with inputs, deliverables, timetable, and acceptance criteria. Separate one-off implementation from recurring software fees. Reuse templates and automate repeated work after learning from real delivery.

**What tends to fail:** Calling labour 'software revenue' hides capacity limits. Unlimited support and loosely defined revisions make a seemingly profitable price expensive. Founder delivery time must be costed even before anyone draws a salary.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: a $1,500 setup requires 12 hours at a planning rate of $60 plus $80 tools. That leaves $700 before sales and overhead. A separate $99/month app fee must cover its own continuing costs.

**What to measure:** Track contribution per project, delivery hours, rework, time to customer value, software retention, and available capacity. Growth without more capacity can reduce quality.

**Distribution implications:** Existing clients, warm referrals, targeted outreach, case studies, and educational videos are practical starting channels. Demonstrate the result and the implementation path together.

**First experiment:** Turn one service engagement into a repeatable package. Record every step and identify which work could become a product feature.

**Further reading:** Metabase sells professional services alongside its software. See [Metabase professional services](https://www.metabase.com/product/professional-services).

<a id="business-13-outcome-based"></a>

## 13. Outcome-based

**How it works:** Payment is triggered by an agreed result, such as a qualified appointment or accepted resolution. Define the metric, timeframe, attribution, and quality threshold before delivery. It can be repeated per outcome; a compelling outcome promise alone does not make a fixed fee outcome-based.

**Best-fit apps:** Workflows with repeatable, observable success and reasonably controllable delivery: resolved support cases, verified processing results, or agreed qualification events.

**What tends to work:** Agree what counts, who verifies it, and how disputed or reversed results are treated. Price the failed attempts needed to produce each successful result. A base fee plus outcome charge can share risk when customers control part of the process.

**What tends to fail:** Vague 'pay for growth' promises invite disputes. Revenue uplift may depend on many factors outside the app. An easy proxy can encourage poor behaviour: booked meetings are not necessarily suitable buyers.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: 200 attempts costing $0.30 each produce 120 accepted outcomes at $1.50. Revenue is $180; attempt cost is $60; contribution is $120 before review, support, and overhead. Cost per outcome is $0.50, not $0.30.

**What to measure:** Track accepted outcomes, success rate, total cost per accepted outcome, disputes, reversals, and customer value. Check whether the billed metric stays aligned with actual satisfaction.

**Distribution implications:** Proof-led demos, case studies, targeted outreach, and referral introductions help buyers trust the measurement. Pilot on a narrow workflow before selling a broad guarantee.

**First experiment:** Define a billable event and a non-billable near miss in writing. Run a sample with the customer and compare independent scoring.

**Further reading:** Intercom documents billable Fin outcomes, including explicit definitions and exceptions. See [Fin outcomes](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/8205718-fin-ai-agent-outcomes). Use the definitions as a worked example, not a universal standard.

## 14. Donations / patronage

**How it works:** Supporters voluntarily fund a useful project. Payments may be one-off or recurring. If payment is required for access to a particular product benefit, that element behaves more like a sale or membership than a pure donation.

**Best-fit apps:** Community utilities, public-interest projects, open-source maintenance, and tools supported by a committed niche.

**What tends to work:** Explain who benefits, what funding sustains, and what each milestone makes possible. Share useful progress and make support easy. Reputation and trust help; being a prominent industry figure is not a prerequisite.

**What tends to fail:** Goodwill is not a predictable revenue floor. Too many bespoke supporter benefits create a service business by accident. Large sponsors can create pressure that conflicts with the project's audience.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: 80 supporters at $5/month produce $400 before platform costs and project expenses. Compare that with maintenance hours, and model what happens if the largest sponsors leave.

**What to measure:** Track recurring supporters, renewals, funding concentration, delivery commitments, and maintenance coverage. Downloads are not the denominator that matters if only a small engaged group supports the project.

**Distribution implications:** Community participation, public development updates, useful documentation, and genuine advocacy fit this model. Ask after someone has experienced value, not only during a funding emergency.

**First experiment:** Write a concrete support request: 'Fund maintenance of this tool for this community.' Test whether users understand what their contribution changes.

**Further reading:** GitHub Sponsors allows one-time and monthly sponsorship tiers. See [GitHub Sponsors documentation](https://docs.github.com/en/sponsors/getting-started-with-github-sponsors/about-github-sponsors).

## 15. Supports another business

**How it works:** The app creates value indirectly by attracting customers, improving retention, or reducing service costs in another business. The user may never pay for the app itself. Its commercial outcome lives in the wider customer journey.

**Best-fit apps:** Calculators, diagnostics, planning tools, customer portals, and companions to goods, memberships, or professional services.

**What tends to work:** Solve a small real problem that naturally precedes or supports the paid offer. Make the next step relevant. For an internal or customer-service tool, measure time or errors saved rather than forcing a lead-generation goal.

**What tends to fail:** Free tools attract unqualified users when the topic is too broad. A form disguised as a tool disappoints people. Large usage can still be a loss if ongoing delivery costs exceed incremental business value.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: a free diagnostic produces 20 qualified enquiries; three become clients, each contributing $600 after service delivery costs. That is $1,800 potential contribution before the tool's acquisition and operating costs. Estimate how many would have bought anyway.

**What to measure:** Track qualified progression to the main offer, incremental conversion, retention or service savings, and app cost. Use comparison groups or staged rollout when practical; correlation alone overstates impact.

**Distribution implications:** SEO around an adjacent problem, existing customer communication, useful community resources, and partnerships can bring suitable users. The free tool should attract the same buyer as the paid offer.

**First experiment:** Map 'free result → next relevant problem → paid offer'. Interview users who use the tool but never need the main business.

**Further reading:** Shopify's free business-name generator offers a path into its commerce product. See [Shopify's generator](https://www.shopify.com/tools/business-name-generator). Its existence does not establish the tool's incremental revenue.

## 16. Lifetime offers

**How it works:** A single payment buys the specified entitlement for the promised lifetime. It is a one-time purchase variant, included separately because its economics deserve their own check. Define whose or what lifetime applies, features, limits, hosting, updates, and support before selling.

**Best-fit apps:** Low ongoing-cost utilities and carefully capped software offers. Can fund early development and bring engaged users; weak fit for unbounded compute-heavy services.

**What tends to work:** Model years of service and full usage, including power users. Reserve part of proceeds for delivery. Set limits and future upgrade boundaries clearly in the original offer, rather than changing the promise after purchase.

**What tends to fail:** Launch cash can disguise a growing service obligation. Discount audiences may differ from recurring subscribers. Feedback can be valuable but support demand may be high. Do not assume the next launch will finance the last cohort.

**Economics example:** Illustrative: $199 received minus $40 channel/fee allowance, $25 onboarding/support, and $72 service cost over a six-year stress scenario leaves $62 before overhead. Six years is a test horizon, not permission to end a lifetime promise then.

**What to measure:** Track net proceeds after refunds, activated customers, service cost per cohort, support burden, and outstanding obligations. Lifetime sales are not monthly recurring revenue.

**Distribution implications:** Relevant deal marketplaces, niche newsletters, and existing audiences can work when the terms and economics fit. Evaluate cohort quality, not just launch revenue.

**First experiment:** Run low, expected, and heavy-use cost scenarios. Cap an initial offer by a delivery budget and learn before expanding.
