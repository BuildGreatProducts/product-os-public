# BONUS - Distribution Channels

*A bonus asset for ProductOS — the twelve ways a first-time builder gets a product in front of strangers, which one to start with, and what "working" actually looks like for each.*

[BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) tells you *which software* to run. This doc comes first — it tells you *which channel to pick* and how to run it. A channel is the road between your product and a stranger's attention. There are only about twelve that matter for a first product, and you do not need all of them. You need **one** that fits your customer, run well enough to produce real revenue, before you touch a second.

> **The meta-rule:** You don't choose your channel. Your customer already chose it. Your only job is to find where they already gather — and show up there before you spread yourself across a second one.

This is the same idea as *Validate by Distribution* from `productos/define/BONUS-Idea-Validation-Cheat-Sheet.md`: if you can't name the exact subreddit, hashtag, search query, or marketplace where your first 100 users already are, the product won't sell no matter how good it is. Distribution isn't what you do after you build — it's the bet the build rests on. Your channel choice should fall straight out of the Customer Persona in `docs/DEFINE.md` (where they spend time) and its Pricing Strategy (what your price can afford to spend acquiring them).

## Pick your channel first

### Go where they already are

Every channel below is just a different answer to one question: *where is your customer's attention already pointed?* A weight-cutting app for wrestlers lives in wrestling communities. A programmatic-screenshot API lives in developer search results and integration marketplaces. A quit-vaping app lives on TikTok. The channel is dictated by the customer, not by your preference — the fastest way to waste three months is to pick the channel *you* enjoy instead of the one *they* use.

### Pick one. Maybe two. Never seven.

The single most common first-timer mistake is "I'll do a bit of everything" — a little TikTok, a little SEO, a launch, some cold emails, a referral program. Seven channels at 10% effort each all fail quietly. One channel at 100% effort produces signal you can actually read. Choose **one primary channel** and run it until it produces repeatable revenue (hit the pass threshold in its section), then — and only then — add a second.

### The decision tree

First, the shape: if your product's shape has a native store, marketplace, or registry (see [Shape-native channels](#shape-native-channels)), that native channel is one of your three by default. The tree then picks the channel that sends its first users.

Start at the top. Stop at the first "yes." This points you at a *starting* channel; the amplifiers (launch platforms, partnerships, referrals, ads) come later, once the starter channel is working.

**Is your product visually demonstrable in under 60 seconds — a before/after, a satisfying result, a relatable problem?**→ **Short-form content.** Short-form video (TikTok/Reels/Shorts) is the highest-leverage free channel for B2C and prosumer apps. Pair it with Communities.

**Does your customer already gather in a nameable place — a subreddit, Discord, Facebook group, or niche forum?**→ **Communities.** Post where they already complain. The cheapest, most direct channel that exists.

**Do people actively *search* for the problem you solve ("how do I…", "best X for Y", a converter/calculator query)?**→ **Search (SEO & GEO).** Slow to start, but durable traffic that doesn't depend on an algorithm's mood. Plant your flag in AI answers now too.

**Are you willing to teach and play a longer game — tutorials, a newsletter, a podcast — for compounding authority and search traffic?**→ **Long-form content.** Slower than short-form, but it builds trust and ranks. A strong second channel, and a natural fit for prosumer and B2B.

**Does your product extend a platform people already live in — Notion, Airtable, Shopify, Chrome, the App Store, WordPress?**→ **Platform ecosystems.** Ride their distribution and their trust. Often the single best channel for a first app.

**Is it higher-ticket (>$100/mo) or sold to businesses, with a findable list of buyers?**→ **Outreach.** Cold and warm DMs and email. The only channel where 50 sends can make rent.

**Do creators your customer already follows exist, and would a 60-second demo from them land?**→ **Influencer / creator.** Borrow an audience you can't build fast enough yourself. Start with cheap micro-creators.

**Already have an audience or an email list — a newsletter, a waitlist, or signups you can message directly — and want to convert and keep them?**→ **Email & lifecycle.** The channel you own. Rarely your first, but the highest-ROI way to turn the traffic every other channel sends into activated, repeat customers.

**None of the above, or you're stuck?**→ Run *#2 Reddit Pain Mining* from `productos/define/BONUS-Idea-Validation-Cheat-Sheet.md` to find where they gather, then come back to the top.

### The fit matrix

| Channel | Best for | Price band it supports | Time to first signal | Ongoing cost | Difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short-form content | B2C, prosumer, visual | Any (great at $5–30/mo) | Days–weeks | Free (your time) | Medium |
| Long-form content | Prosumer, B2B, authority | Any | Weeks–months | Free–low | Medium–High |
| Communities | B2C, prosumer, niche B2B | Any | Days | Free | Low–Medium |
| Search (SEO & GEO) | Everyone, esp. prosumer/B2B | Any | 3–6 months | Free–low | Medium–High |
| Platform ecosystems | Prosumer, B2B | Any | Weeks | Free–platform fee | Medium |
| Launch platforms | Everyone (one-shot) | Any (great for LTDs) | One day | Free–platform fee | Low |
| Outreach | B2B, high-ticket | $100/mo+ | Days | Low (tools) | Medium |
| Influencer / creator | B2C | $5–30/mo | Weeks | Per-post fees | Medium |
| Partnerships | Prosumer, B2B | Any | Weeks–months | Rev-share | Medium–High |
| Email & lifecycle | Everyone (once you have a list) | Any | Days | Free–low | Low–Medium |
| Referrals & affiliates | Everyone (once you have users) | Any | Months | Rev-share | Medium |
| Ads | Everyone (once the funnel converts) | LTV must cover CAC | Days | $$ ongoing | High |

The channels roughly fall into four families: **organic pull** (short-form, long-form, communities, search, platform ecosystems — you make something findable), **direct push** (outreach — you go to them), **borrowed audience** (influencer, partnerships, launch platforms — someone else's reach), and **compounding loops** (email, referrals, ads — owned and paid engines that multiply what already works). First revenue almost always comes from the organic-pull family. The rest amplify it.

## The twelve channels

---

### 1. Short-form content

*Organic short-form video — TikTok, Reels, and YouTube Shorts that earn attention for free and funnel viewers to your product.*

The highest-leverage starting channel for most first-time founders: free, compounding, and people buy from people. Short-form is the discovery engine — you win on the hook, copy proven formats, and post at volume. Best place to start if you're pre-revenue and your product is at all visual or demonstrable.

**Best fit:** B2C and prosumer apps, especially consumer subscriptions at $5–30/mo with a visual or relatable hook. Reach for it when the problem can be shown in a single clip.

**How to start:** Find the viral content already working in your niche (search the hashtag, sort by most-liked) and copy the *format* — hook, beats, CTA — with your own demo. Lead the first 2–3 seconds with the customer's pain, not your product. Post one a day for two weeks before judging; deep-link to the feature, never the homepage.

**What good looks like:** One post clears ~100K organic views and drives 50+ trial signups within your first 2 weeks of daily posting.

**Watch out for:** Posting once and quitting — the algorithm rewards volume and iteration. And don't confuse views with revenue; a clip that doesn't deep-link to a buy action is a vanity number.

> **Real example — Steven Cravada, PuffCount, $40K/mo.** Scrolled `#quitvaping`, found a 3.7M-like "vape-in-water" video, and rebuilt that exact format around his own app. Organic TikTok did 50M+ views and built the entire business *before* he spent a dollar on ads.

**Tools:** [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §1 (Organic social & short-form video).

### 2. Long-form content

*YouTube tutorials, podcasts, newsletters, and long-form posts — depth that builds authority, trust, and searchable assets that compound for years.*

Where short-form wins attention, long-form earns trust and ranks. A how-to video built around one search query keeps converting for months; a build-in-public thread or newsletter turns your journey into an audience; a podcast borrows another host's listeners. Slower than short-form, but it compounds harder and reaches buyers short-form can't.

**Best fit:** Prosumer and B2B products, and any founder willing to teach and play a longer game. Especially strong where buyers research before they buy. Any price band.

**How to start:** Pick the question your customer asks most and answer it — a focused tutorial (cut the long intro, built around one search phrase), a long-form written story (a result, a lesson) on X / LinkedIn or a newsletter, or a guest spot on a podcast your customer already listens to. Hold a weekly cadence and feed it with the hooks that won in short-form.

**What good looks like:** One asset drives a steady trickle of signups for months — e.g., a problem-specific tutorial that converts 3+ paying customers over 90 days even at a few hundred views.

**Watch out for:** Expecting short-form speed — long-form pays off in months, not days. And rambling: each asset should answer one thing, fast.

> **Real example — Vikash, Bulk Mockup, $12K/mo.** A YouTube "content flywheel" of narrow how-to videos answering exact customer pains; a single 370-view video drove $345 MRR from 3 customers.

> **Real example — Louis Pereira, AudioPen, $15K/mo.** Built the whole audience in writing — building in public on Twitter, sharing the product and the numbers until strangers asked to pay.

**Tools:** [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §1–2 and §7 (video editing, SEO/content, newsletters).

### 3. Communities

*Posting and participating where your customer already gathers — subreddits, Discords, Facebook groups, niche forums.*

The most direct channel that exists: instead of building an audience, you borrow one that's already assembled around the exact problem you solve. Often free, often the source of a first product's first 100 customers. Works best for tightly-defined niches under ~100K people.

**Best fit:** B2C, prosumer, and niche B2B where you can name a specific community of fewer than 100K people who hang out together online. Any price band.

**How to start:** Map 10–20 communities where your ICP lives. *Lurk first* — read for a week, learn the exact vocabulary they use for their pain. Then contribute value before you ever link your product (answer questions, share the workaround). When you do post, frame it as "I built this for myself because X frustrated me," not as an ad. Set keyword alerts (e.g. F5Bot) so you can show up the moment someone describes your problem.

**What good looks like:** 20+ paying users from a single community within 30 days, with referrals starting inside the group on their own.

**Watch out for:** Spamming a link on day one and getting banned — communities punish takers and reward givers. And one delighted commenter is a fan, not a market; you need repeated, unrelated pulls.

> **Real example — Ovnish, Save Wise, $25K/mo.** Lurked in credit-card and Rakuten-stacking Facebook groups for weeks, copied the members' exact vocabulary into his positioning, then posted where the points-obsessed already argued. Reddit plus those groups carried the business with zero ad spend.

> **Real example — Julien Nahum, Note Forms, $37K/mo.** Built a form builder specifically for Notion users and seeded it in Notion subreddits and Facebook groups for the first wave — which then turned into a viral loop.

**Tools:** Mostly manual. For listening at scale, see [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §1 (social listening).

### 4. Search — SEO & GEO

*Owning the queries your customer types — into Google (SEO) and now into AI answer engines (GEO/AEO).*

Slow to start, but the most durable channel there is: traffic that doesn't depend on an algorithm's daily mood and compounds for years. **SEO** is ranking for what people search on Google. **GEO** (Generative Engine Optimization, also called AEO) is the newer, fast-rising sibling — getting named when buyers ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Gemini for a recommendation. Same logic (own the query), different surface. The data you collect in AI answers today becomes your baseline, so plant a flag now.

**Best fit:** Everyone, but especially prosumer and B2B tools where buyers search with intent ("best X for Y", "how to do Z", a converter or calculator). Any price band.

**How to start:** Pick the handful of high-intent queries closest to a purchase, not the high-volume vanity ones. The highest-converting move for a first app is a **free single-purpose tool** (a converter, calculator, generator) with no signup gate that ranks for its keyword and feeds users into the paid product. For GEO, run a free AI-visibility audit to see how the engines describe you today, then create the comparison and "best tool for…" content they cite.

**What good looks like:** One page or free tool ranking top-3 for its keyword within ~3 months, converting 15–20% of visitors to signup; for GEO, your brand named in AI answers for your core category query.

**Watch out for:** Expecting results in week 3 — SEO pays off in month 3+, so don't pivot away from a product because search is "slow." And chasing traffic that never buys; rank for intent, not volume.

> **Real example — Angus Cheng, BankStatementConverter.com, $40K MRR.** Ranked for exactly what accountants search to convert a bank statement into a spreadsheet. ~40K visits a month of pure purchase intent, no social presence required.

> **Real example — Joseph, Super Demo, $250K/mo.** Built dozens of free, ungated adjacent tools (screenshot makers, SOP builders); those free tools now drive ~20% of all traffic into the paid product.

**Tools:** [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §2 (SEO & content) and §3 (GEO / AEO).

### 5. Platform ecosystems

*Building on top of a platform people already live in — and using its marketplace, store, and trust as your distribution.*

One of the highest-leverage and most underrated channels for a first product: instead of acquiring an audience, you attach to one. Notion, Airtable, Shopify, Chrome, the iOS/Mac App Stores, WordPress, Figma, the GPT store — each is a marketplace of millions of users actively looking for tools that extend what they already use. Their search, their store, and their credibility become yours.

**Best fit:** Prosumer and B2B tools that naturally extend a host product or fit an app-store keyword cluster. Any price band.

**How to start:** Pick *one* platform your customer already lives in and build the narrowest useful extension. List it in that platform's marketplace/store and optimize the listing for how users there search (App Store Optimization is the mobile version of SEO — keywords, screenshots, ratings). Lean into the platform's own communities to seed the first reviews, which then drive the marketplace ranking.

**What good looks like:** Your listing ranking for its core in-marketplace keyword and producing steady installs/trials without you posting daily — the platform's traffic doing the work.

**Watch out for:** Platform risk — you're a tenant, not an owner, so a policy or algorithm change can cut your supply overnight. Treat it as a powerful *starter* channel and use it to build an owned email list you control.

> **Real example — Andy Cloke, Data Fetcher, $23K MRR.** Built a data extension *inside* Airtable and listed it in Airtable's marketplace, landing in front of Airtable's users (Amazon, Harvard, Stanford among them) with no audience of his own.

> **Real example — Katie Keith, Barn2, ~$150K/mo.** A portfolio of 19 WordPress/WooCommerce plugins grown almost entirely on WordPress-ecosystem SEO and customer feature requests — same buyer, many products, one platform's gravity.

**Tools:** Platform-native (each store's own listing and ASO tools). See [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §2 for the SEO/ASO overlap.

### 6. Launch platforms

*The one-shot spike — Product Hunt, AppSumo / RocketHub, Hacker News, app-store features.*

Distinct from every other channel here: a launch is a **moment, not a channel.** Done well it delivers a concentrated burst of traffic, first reviews, backlinks, and (on LTD platforms) a cash injection plus a cohort of evangelists. It does not sustain a business — but it's a great way to ignite one and to convert a finished build into early customers and social proof.

**Best fit:** Everyone, once. Especially strong for products that suit a lifetime deal (productivity SaaS, Mac apps, one-time-purchase tools) and for founders without an existing audience — the platform brings its own buyer list.

**How to start:** Pick the platform that matches your buyer — Product Hunt (makers, tech-curious), Hacker News (developers, technical), AppSumo/RocketHub (deal-seeking SaaS buyers). Line up your assets in advance (demo video, first comments, a real price), notify whatever small audience you have to land early momentum, and treat the day as a sprint. For LTD platforms, tier the offer and engineer a middle default.

**What good looks like:** $10K+ in real card swipes in an LTD window (with 20–25 of those buyers active weekly), or a launch that nets durable backlinks, reviews, and a few hundred evangelists you can re-activate.

**Watch out for:** Treating the launch as the strategy — the spike fades in 48 hours, so have a steady channel ready to catch the attention. And don't launch to an empty room: a launch amplifies existing momentum, it doesn't manufacture it.

> **Real example — Devin, Supergrow, $230K/year.** Ran a 3-day lifetime deal on RocketHub with $79 / $199 / $299 tiers, made $65K in 3 days, and kept ~250 paying evangelists who reviewed and referred.

> **Real example — Blaine, Castmagic, $120K+ MRR.** An AppSumo launch brought in ~10,000 users and roughly $350K — a standing-start cohort that seeded everything after.

**Tools:** No software needed — the platforms named above are the channel.

### 7. Outreach

*Going directly to named buyers — cold and warm DMs, cold email, manual one-to-one selling.*

The channel where you don't wait to be found — you build a list of exact buyers and contact them. Modern outreach splits into **signals/data** (who to contact and when) and **sending** (getting the message read). It's manual and unglamorous, but it's the only channel where 50 well-aimed messages can produce real revenue this week, which makes it ideal for higher-ticket and B2B products.

**Best fit:** B2B and high-ticket products ($100/mo and up) where a buyer is worth enough to justify a personal message and you can name the companies or roles you're selling to.

**How to start:** Define the exact ICP (role, company type, trigger event), build a small high-quality list, and personalize the first line to something real about *them* — not a mail-merge token. Lead with a specific outcome or a non-monetary commitment ask (a call, a confirmed date) rather than a hard pitch. Send in small batches, watch reply rates, and iterate the message before scaling volume.

**What good looks like:** A repeatable response→call→close rate from cold sends — concretely, your first handful of paying customers (or 10+ hard commitments) traceable directly to outreach within a few weeks.

**Watch out for:** Blasting a generic template to 5,000 addresses — it kills deliverability and your domain reputation, and teaches you nothing. Volume only works *after* a personalized version converts.

> **Real example — Brian Shin, Once, $20K/mo in 83 days.** DM'd ~300 strangers through wedding and birthday hashtags and required 10 confirmed event dates before writing any code. Outreach was both his validation and his first revenue.

> **Real example — Marcos, Birdhouse, $65K/mo.** Cold, warm, and hot DMs on Twitter — a disciplined one-to-one motion — paired with content, took it past $60K/mo.

**Tools:** [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §4 (Outreach & lead generation).

### 8. Influencer & creator

*Borrowing a creator's audience — paying or gifting people who already have your customer's trust to demo the product.*

When the audience you need already follows someone else, you can rent that trust instead of spending months building your own. Ranges from cheap micro-creator collaborations to full UGC pipelines. Especially powerful for B2C, where a single authentic demo from the right creator can outperform weeks of your own posting.

**Best fit:** B2C consumer apps at $5–30/mo with a visual, relatable hook and creators who already serve your exact demographic.

**How to start:** Start with **micro-creators**, not big names — they're cheap (often ~$100–150 a video), more authentic, and let you test many hooks fast. Find creators whose audience *is* your ICP, brief them on the problem (let them keep their own voice), and run many small placements rather than one expensive bet. Track which creator and which hook drives installs, then double down.

**What good looks like:** A repeatable cost-per-install from creator placements that's below your payback threshold, with at least one creator/hook combination you can re-run profitably.

**Watch out for:** Paying a big creator for one vanity placement with no tracking. And mismatched audiences — a huge following that isn't your customer converts worse than a tiny one that is.

> **Real example — Kelechi, Social Wizard, $60K/mo.** Paid micro-streamers about $120 a video to demo the app to their followers — cheap, scalable, and aimed squarely at the 16–24 year-olds he was selling to.

> **Real example — Blake Anderson, Cal AI, $1M+/mo.** Scaled a calorie-tracking app primarily on influencer marketing and UGC — the right creators showing the magic moment to exactly the right audience.

**Tools:** [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §6 (Influencer & creator marketing).

### 9. Partnerships

*Teaming up with another product or audience-owner — integrations, co-marketing, bundles, and audience-borrowing deals.*

A leverage channel: instead of one-to-one outreach, you strike a deal that puts you in front of an entire audience or user base at once. Three common shapes — **integrations** (be part of a tool your customer already uses, so their users discover you), **co-marketing** (a creator, course, or company promotes you to their audience), and **bundles** (package with a complementary product). Slower to set up than posting, but each deal can be worth thousands of customers.

**Best fit:** Prosumer and B2B products with a natural complement or a clear "their audience is my ICP" partner. Any price band.

**How to start:** List the products and audience-owners your customer already trusts. For integrations, build the connector (Zapier/Make or a native one) so you appear in *their* ecosystem. For audience deals, lead with what's in it for *them* (revenue share, a co-branded asset, a genuinely useful integration) — partnerships die when only one side benefits. Start with one warm partner and prove the motion before scaling.

**What good looks like:** One partnership producing a steady, attributable stream of signups or revenue you didn't have to chase one by one — repeatable enough to go find three more like it.

**Watch out for:** Vague "let's cross-promote" handshakes that never ship. Pin down who does what, by when, and how it's tracked, or it evaporates.

> **Real example — Hassam, LaunchFast, $21.8K MRR in 90 days.** Partnered with the Legacy X coaching program to put his product in front of their audience of Amazon sellers — a warm, trusting audience borrowed wholesale instead of built cold.

> **Real example — Dmytro, Screenshot One, $12K/mo.** Built Zapier and Make integrations so users of *those* platforms discovered the product inside the tools they already ran.

**Tools:** [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §6 and §8 (creator partnerships & all-in-one GTM).

### 10. Email & lifecycle

*The owned channel — a direct line to your audience (a newsletter and triggered lifecycle sequences) that converts and retains the traffic every other channel sends, and compounds into its own growth loop.*

Every other channel rents attention from an algorithm or a platform; email is the one you own. It rarely finds strangers — you build the list through the other channels — but it's the highest-ROI channel for turning one-time visitors into activated users and repeat buyers, and a newsletter worth forwarding becomes an acquisition loop of its own. Set it up early; it compounds while rented attention resets.

**Best fit:** Everyone, the moment you're capturing any signups or have traffic to convert. Especially strong for content/audience-first, prosumer, and B2B products. Any price band.

**How to start:** Capture emails from day one (a signup, a waitlist, a lead magnet). Wire the two highest-leverage sequences first — a **welcome series** that walks a new signup to the magic moment (the single highest-ROI email you'll send) and a **win-back** for people who go quiet — then run a regular **broadcast** that earns the open. Deep-link every email to the magic moment, segment by behavior as you grow, and never buy a list.

**What good looks like:** A welcome series that measurably lifts activation, a broadcast holding a healthy open and click rate, and email driving a steady, attributable share of conversions and reactivations each month.

**Watch out for:** Treating email as a megaphone — blasting one broadcast to everyone — instead of a behavioral engine; the money is in triggered, segmented sequences. And building a list you never message: an unworked list decays fast.

> **Real example — Alex Lieberman, Morning Brew, $75M+/yr.** Built a media business on a single owned channel — a daily newsletter — and grew it with a referral program that turned readers into the acquisition engine. The list *was* the business.

> **Real example — Dustin, Magai, ~$100K/mo.** A 100K-subscriber email list, built off a personal brand, is the engine that converts and re-sells across his products — the owned audience compounds where rented attention doesn't.

**Tools:** [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §7 (Email & lifecycle marketing).

### 11. Referrals & affiliates

*Turning the users and promoters you already have into a channel — word of mouth, built-in viral loops, and paid affiliate programs.*

The channel that only switches on once you have something worth talking about — which is exactly why it's not a *starting* channel. Three forms: **word of mouth** (users tell users), **built-in loops** (the product spreads itself through normal use), and **affiliates** (you pay a commission to people who send buyers). When it works, it's the cheapest growth there is because your customers and partners do the distributing.

**Best fit:** Everyone, *after* you have happy users and a product people would actually recommend. Strongest where usage is visible to non-users or the niche is tight-knit.

**How to start:** First, earn it — referrals only compound on genuine retention, so fix the product before you build the program. Then engineer the loop: add a light "powered by you" surface to anything users share, give existing users a reason and an easy way to invite, and for affiliates, offer a clear recurring commission to creators and operators whose audience is your ICP.

**What good looks like:** A measurable share of new signups arriving from existing users or affiliates without paid spend — a referral/viral coefficient that's climbing, or affiliates producing steady attributable revenue.

**Watch out for:** Bolting a referral program onto a product nobody loves yet — incentives can't manufacture word of mouth that the product hasn't earned. And affiliate programs need margin to fund the commission; check it against the Business Strategy section of `docs/DEFINE.md` (run `define-business-strategy` before funding commissions).

> **Real example — Lucas Herman, Stagetimer, $25K/mo.** Put a small "powered by Stagetimer" logo on every shared timer. Every event the product ran became an ad seen by the next event's organizer — roughly a third of the business came from that built-in loop, no formal program required.

> **Real example — Dustin, Magai, ~$100K/mo.** Layered a recurring-commission affiliate program on top of a 100K-person email list, paying promoters to bring buyers he'd never have reached alone.

**Tools:** See [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §7 (email & lifecycle) for the retention engine referrals depend on.

### 12. Ads

*Paying to put proven messaging in front of more of the right people — Meta, Google, TikTok, Apple Search Ads.*

The last channel to reach for, not the first. Ads are an **amplifier, not an igniter** — they pour fuel on a fire that's already lit. If you can't get organic conversion in the niche where your customers live, ads will burn cash and teach you nothing. But once you have a hook that converts and a funnel that pays back, ads are the fastest way to scale what's already working. The real constraint now is creative volume: winning ads fatigue in weeks.

**Best fit:** Everyone, but *only* once organic has proven the message and your unit economics (LTV vs. CAC — run `define-business-strategy` before spending) can support paid acquisition.

**How to start:** Don't invent ad creative — promote the organic content that already worked. Take your best-performing post or demo, put a small budget behind it to one tightly-defined audience, and point it at a dedicated landing page (not the homepage). Measure cost-per-acquisition against payback, kill losers fast, and scale only the creative that profits.

**What good looks like:** A repeatable, positive-margin acquisition loop — spend $X, reliably get back more than $X in customer value within your payback window — that you can scale by adding budget and fresh creative.

**Watch out for:** Running ads to fix a funnel that doesn't convert organically — that's setting money on fire. And creative fatigue: budget without a steady supply of new hooks decays fast.

> **Real example — Steven Cravada, PuffCount, $40K/mo.** Turned on TikTok and Meta ads *only after* organic TikTok had already proven the hook and the funnel. Ads scaled a fire that was already burning — they didn't start it.

> **Real example — Nico, Make Logo AI, ~$85K in 6 months.** Went fully organic first, then ran Facebook *static* ads into audience-specific landing pages once he knew which message converted.

**Tools:** [BONUS - AI Distribution Tools](BONUS-AI-Distribution-Tools.md) §5 (Paid ads — creative & management).

## Shape-native channels

Every product shape (see `productos/shapes/SHAPES.md`; the primary shape is named in `docs/DEFINE.md` → `## Product Shape`) has places where its customers already go *looking for that kind of thing*: a store, a marketplace, a registry, a directory. That is the shape's **native channel**. It's usually a version of #5 Platform ecosystems or #4 Search, and it's the one channel a product of that shape can't afford to skip, because it's where intent already lives. A browser extension nobody can find in the Chrome Web Store has to buy every user it gets; one that ranks for its core keyword gets installs while its maker sleeps.

### The native-channel rule

- **One of your three channels is the shape's native channel**, unless there's a stated reason it isn't (the store bans the category, the product sells only to a named list of enterprise buyers, the member already owns a stronger channel). Say the reason out loud; don't drop it silently.
- **It's rarely enough alone.** Native discovery rewards products that already have installs, reviews, and usage. Pair it with a channel that sends the first users (communities, outreach, a launch), whose installs and reviews then lift the native ranking.
- **Your listing is the landing page.** On a store or marketplace, the listing (name, short description, images, first lines, reviews) does the job a landing page does on the web. `design-app-listing` writes App Store / Google Play listings; `design-marketplace-listing` writes every other store, marketplace, or registry listing.
- **Build the owned list anyway.** You're a tenant on every store. Capture an email or account at first use so a policy or ranking change can't cut you off from the customers you already have.

### How discovery works on any store or directory

The mechanics differ by platform, but the same five forces decide who gets found almost everywhere. Optimize them in this order:

1. **Relevance** — the words in the name, short description, keywords and categories match what customers type. Use the customer's words from the Persona, not your category jargon.
2. **Conversion** — of the people who see the listing, how many install, add, or buy. Images, the first line, and the price shown do most of this work.
3. **Social proof** — the number, recency, and average of ratings and reviews. Most stores weight recent reviews, so a steady trickle beats one launch-day burst.
4. **Usage and retention** — installs that stay installed, servers that keep getting called, buyers who don't refund. Stores increasingly read whether people *keep* using what they found.
5. **Curation** — editorial features, badges, "staff picks", official directories. Usually earned by quality and policy compliance, often requested through a form, never bought.

Directories and registries without a ranking algorithm (a plain list, a GitHub "awesome" list, a registry that only records metadata) reward the first two forces plus being *listed at all* — get into every legitimate one the shape's customers browse, and keep the entry current.

### By shape — last reviewed October 2026, re-verify before quoting

Store names, fees, badges, review rules, and the existence of newer marketplaces change often; agent-tool marketplaces especially. Check each platform's current documentation before quoting any of this to a member.

#### `web-app`

- **Native places:** search (Google and AI answers) is the native channel; then launch platforms (Product Hunt, Hacker News "Show HN"); software review directories for B2B (G2, Capterra); AI-tool directories for AI products; and the integration directories of tools it connects to (Zapier, Slack, HubSpot, Shopify app listings).
- **Discovery:** search ranks pages by intent match and authority (links, depth on the topic); review directories rank by review count, recency and category fit; launch platforms rank on a single day's votes and comments.
- **First move:** one page per high-intent search ("[job] tool for [persona]", "[competitor] alternative"), and a listing in the integration directory of the platform the persona already uses.

#### `mobile-app`

- **Native places:** Apple's App Store and Google Play.
- **Discovery:** App Store search reads the app name, subtitle, and a hidden keyword field; Google Play reads the title, short description and full description. Both weight install velocity, ratings and reviews, and conversion from listing view to install; Google Play also reads uninstalls and engagement. Editorial features are requested (Apple has a featuring nomination form in App Store Connect). Both stores offer native listing tests (Apple's product page optimization and custom product pages; Google Play's store listing experiments).
- **Fees (dated):** Apple's Developer Program is $99 a year and Google Play registration is a one-time $25; both take 15% of digital sales for small developers (Apple's Small Business Program, Google's first $1M a year) and up to 30% otherwise. Rules for linking out to web payments have been changing by region since 2024–2025; check the current guidelines.
- **First move:** keyword research for the persona's search terms, a listing written by `design-app-listing`, and a review prompt placed right after the magic moment.

#### `desktop-app`

- **Native places:** the Mac App Store and Microsoft Store; Setapp (a curated Mac subscription bundle you apply to); Homebrew casks for technical buyers; software-alternative directories (AlternativeTo) and Mac-app curators; Product Hunt and Hacker News for launches.
- **Discovery:** desktop store search is weaker than mobile, so most desktop apps sell direct — a website, search, and a launch — and use the stores as a trust signal. Alternative directories rank by user likes against a named incumbent ("alternative to X").
- **First move:** an "alternative to [incumbent]" listing on AlternativeTo and a comparison page on the site; apply to Setapp if the app is Mac-only and subscription-friendly.

#### `browser-extension`

- **Native places:** the Chrome Web Store (also serves Edge, Brave, Arc and other Chromium browsers), Microsoft Edge Add-ons, Firefox Add-ons, and Safari extensions via the Mac App Store.
- **Discovery:** store search matches the name and short description; ranking weights user count, ratings count and average, and recent updates. Chrome's **Featured** badge is granted by manual review for best practices and a clear listing (request it through Chrome Web Store support); the **Established Publisher** badge comes automatically after identity verification and a clean track record over months. Google says badged extensions *may* rank higher.
- **Fees (dated):** the Chrome Web Store charges a one-time $5 developer registration and has no built-in payments; charge through your own checkout or a licence service.
- **First move:** a listing written by `design-marketplace-listing` around the one job, five screenshots of that job, and a review prompt after the first successful use.

#### `agent-plugin`

- **Native places:** Anthropic's plugin directory (surfaced in Claude Code as the official marketplace and in Cowork; submission by in-app form, reviewed), any git repo published as a Claude Code plugin marketplace (`/plugin marketplace add owner/repo`), the Cursor Marketplace (manually reviewed; community plugins on cursor.directory), Codex's plugin listings, GitHub itself, and community-curated "awesome" lists for each tool. The communities are the tools' own Discords, subreddits, and X/LinkedIn circles.
- **Discovery:** official directories are curated, not ranked by an algorithm — getting listed is the win. Outside them, discovery is GitHub stars, inclusion in curated lists, word of mouth in the tool's community, and demos of the plugin doing one job end to end. A cross-tool plugin (one repo installable in several agents) multiplies the reachable audience.
- **First move:** a public repo with a README that shows the first successful output in under a minute, submission to each official directory the plugin supports, and a demo post in the tool's main community.

#### `agent-skill`

- **Native places:** Anthropic's skills directory and the `anthropics/skills` repo; skills bundled into a plugin (then every `agent-plugin` place applies); third-party skill directories built on the open Agent Skills format published at agentskills.io (several exist; catalogue sizes and security review vary widely — some scrape GitHub with no review); community "awesome" lists.
- **Discovery:** mostly curation and word of mouth. Agents choose a skill by its description at run time, so the description is also the in-product ranking signal: a skill whose description matches the member's request gets used, one that doesn't sits idle.
- **First move:** ship the skill inside a small plugin so it installs in one command, list it in the official directory and one reputable third-party directory, and demo the before/after output in the persona's own community (not only AI-builder circles).

#### `mcp-server`

- **Native places:** the official MCP Registry (registry.modelcontextprotocol.io — an open metadata registry, still labelled a preview, that requires namespace verification; other directories build on it), GitHub's MCP registry, Anthropic's connectors directory for Claude, ChatGPT's app directory (apps built on MCP with the Apps SDK, reviewed by OpenAI), the Cursor Marketplace and cursor.directory, and independent directories (Smithery, PulseMCP, Glama, mcp.so). Also the integration pages of the system it connects to.
- **Discovery:** the official registry only records that a server exists; ranking happens in the directories and clients built on top, which weight usage, GitHub stars, recency, and curation. Listing in a client's own directory (Claude, ChatGPT, Cursor) puts the server in front of users at the moment they add tools.
- **Fees and rules (dated):** ChatGPT apps could not sell digital goods or subscriptions in-app at launch (December 2025); check whether that has changed.
- **First move:** publish to the official registry, submit to the directories of the two clients the persona uses most, and write the integration page the connected system's users will search for ("[system] MCP server").

#### `chat-assistant`

- **Native places:** the GPT Store inside ChatGPT (custom GPTs); ChatGPT's app directory for richer apps; the Slack Marketplace, Microsoft Teams store, and Discord's App Directory for workspace bots (top.gg for community Discord bots); Telegram and WhatsApp have no real store, so their bots travel by link, QR code, and the communities that run on those apps.
- **Discovery:** the GPT Store ranks by usage and ratings within categories, with editorial picks; workspace marketplaces rank by installs, reviews and category, and reward a listing that names the job in the first line. Off-store bots grow only through the communities they serve.
- **First move:** a store listing that names one job, and a "try it" link posted where the persona already talks — the bot used live in front of them is the demo.

#### `developer-tool`

- **Native places:** the package registry for the language (npm, PyPI, crates.io, RubyGems, Go modules), Homebrew for CLIs, GitHub (stars, Trending, README), Docker Hub, API marketplaces and networks (RapidAPI, Postman's API Network), and the developer communities: Hacker News, the language's subreddit and Discord, dev.to.
- **Discovery:** registries search by package name, keywords and description and surface download counts, recent releases and maintenance signals; GitHub Trending ranks star velocity; documentation pages rank in search and increasingly in AI coding assistants' answers.
- **First move:** a README with a copy-paste quickstart that reaches the first successful call in under five minutes, registry keywords in the developer's own terms, and a "Show HN" once a real user has succeeded with it.

#### `productized-service`

- **Native places:** no store; the native channels are **outreach and referrals**. Freelance marketplaces with fixed-price catalogues (Upwork's Project Catalog, Fiverr, Contra, Malt in Europe); partner and expert directories of the platforms the service works on (Shopify Partners, HubSpot Solutions, Webflow Experts, Xero and QuickBooks advisor directories); LinkedIn, where most B2B buyers of services are reachable by name.
- **Discovery:** marketplaces rank sellers by reviews, completion and response rates, repeat clients, and recent earnings — new sellers start at the bottom and climb by delivering. Partner directories rank by certification tier and reviews.
- **First move:** a named list of 30 buyers who match the persona and a fixed-price offer sent personally; a marketplace listing as a second channel, priced to win the first reviews.

#### `website`

- **Native places:** search (Google, Google Discover, AI answers) is the native channel; newsletter recommendation networks (Substack Recommendations, beehiiv's recommendation network and Boosts, SparkLoop); Reddit and the niche's forums, where directories and resources get shared; for directories, the "list of lists" and resource roundups in the niche.
- **Discovery:** search rewards topical depth (many pages answering the niche's questions), internal links, and links from the niche's own sites; recommendation networks reward a newsletter that converts readers it's shown to.
- **First move:** the twenty pages answering the twenty questions the persona searches, and a recommendation swap with two newsletters the same reader already gets.

#### `digital-product`

- **Native places:** marketplaces for the format — Etsy (digital downloads), Gumroad Discover, the Notion Marketplace (templates), Framer and Webflow marketplaces, Figma Community, Creative Market, Envato, Udemy (courses) — plus the creator's own audience, where most sales happen.
- **Discovery:** marketplace search ranks by keyword match in title and tags, listing quality, conversion, and reviews; Gumroad Discover surfaces products that already sell; template marketplaces add editorial features. Marketplace fees are usually far higher than a direct checkout's, which is why creators use marketplaces for discovery and their own page for repeat buyers.
- **First move:** list the product on the one marketplace where buyers of that format search, with preview images of the finished result; send every buyer to an email list for the next product.

## Running more than one channel

Add a second channel only after the first hits its pass threshold and produces revenue you can repeat. When you do, pick an **adjacent** channel that shares an asset with the first, so your effort compounds instead of splitting:

| If your first channel is… | A natural second is… | Why they compound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short-form content | Influencer/creator, then Ads | Same short-form videos fuel all three |
| Long-form content | Search, then Referrals | Tutorials and essays compound into search traffic and word of mouth |
| Communities | Launch platforms, then Referrals | Engaged communities power launch-day spikes and word of mouth |
| Email & lifecycle | Referrals, then Ads | An owned list powers word-of-mouth referrals and warm, high-converting ad audiences |
| Search (SEO/GEO) | Platform ecosystems | Both are "be found at the moment of intent" |
| Outreach | Partnerships | Both are direct relationships, just one-to-one vs. one-to-many |
| Platform ecosystems | Launch platforms, then Search | A launch seeds the reviews that drive marketplace + search ranking |

The deeper logic: **organic proves the message, paid and referral scale it.** Never run ads or build an affiliate program until an organic channel has shown you a hook that converts — you'd just be paying to amplify something that doesn't work yet.

## A starter sequence (by who you're selling to)

| You're selling… | Start with | Then add | Amplify with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A consumer app ($5–30/mo, visual) | Short-form content | Communities | Influencer, then Ads |
| A prosumer tool built on a platform | Platform ecosystems | Communities | A launch-platform moment |
| A niche hobby/community product | Communities | Short-form content | Referrals |
| A B2B or higher-ticket product | Outreach | Search (comparison/SEO) | Partnerships |
| A content/audience-first product | Long-form content | Email & lifecycle | Referrals & affiliates |

Cross-reference your pick against the Pricing Strategy in `docs/DEFINE.md` (and its Business Strategy section if you've filled it) — your price has to fund the channel's cost, and your unfair advantage often *is* owning one channel deeply.

## The most common channel mistakes

**Spreading across seven channels at 10% effort each.** One channel at 100% beats seven at 10% every time. Pick one, go deep, ignore the rest until it's working.

**Jumping to ads before organic converts.** Ads amplify a working funnel; they don't create one. If strangers won't convert for free in the niche where they live, paid traffic just loses money faster.

**Quitting a channel before it compounds.** SEO, communities, and content pay off in month 3, not week 3. Before you abandon a channel — or the product — check `productos/define/BONUS-Pivot-Framework.md` for whether you actually have a distribution-patience problem, not a product problem.

**Picking the channel you like, not the one your customer is on.** Your comfort is not a strategy. The wrestler's app belongs in wrestling communities even if you'd rather write blog posts.

**Optimizing vanity metrics instead of card swipes.** Views, followers, and upvotes feel like progress; only signups and revenue are. Tie every channel to its revenue-linked pass threshold, not its reach.

**Copying another founder's channel without copying their persona.** PuffCount's TikTok works because quitting vaping is visual and emotional. Your B2B API isn't — the channel has to match *your* customer, not the case study's.

**Abandoning a working channel to chase a "scalable" one.** If Reddit is paying rent, run Reddit until it genuinely caps out. Founders routinely leave a channel that works to chase one that sounds bigger, and stall both.

## Final note

Distribution is a discipline, not a launch day. The founders in this dataset who hit first revenue weren't on more channels than everyone else — they were on the *right* one, run deeper than everyone else. Pick the road your customer already walks. Run it until it pays. Then, and only then, build the second.
